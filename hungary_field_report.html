HUNGARY

Canine Dirofilariosis and Molecular Diagnosis

Field Research Report: Clinical Workflows, Species Differentiation, and Market Signals

Field Survey Notes Veterinary Diagnostics Hungary (KOL Clinics)

Executive Summary & Key Diagnostic Challenges

The Hungarian field notes highlight a practical diagnostic challenge: canine infections involving Dirofilaria immitis and Dirofilaria repens may require different interpretations, while the source report states that the rapid antigen tests observed during the visit targeted D. immitis and did not differentiate the two species.

The visits also recorded substantial use of modified Knott's testing at one KOL-associated clinic and a reported increase in annual test volume from 600–700 in 2023 to 998 in 2024. These observations suggest that species differentiation, confirmation pathways, and assay validation are important topics for further investigation.

1. Overview & Zoonotic Significance

Figure: Diagnostic workflow and chart

Cardiovascular Risk

Dirofilaria immitis

Primary Disease: Heartworm Disease (Canine Heartworm)

  • Pathology: Primarily targets and parasitizes the cardiovascular system.
  • Clinical Impact: Leads to severe cardiovascular disease, pulmonary arterial damage, and heart failure.
Subcutaneous & Zoonotic

Dirofilaria repens

Primary Disease: Subcutaneous Dirofilariasis

  • Pathology: Adult worms parasitize subcutaneous tissues long-term.
  • Symptoms: Multifocal alopecia (patchy hair loss) and skin breakdown/ulceration.
  • Clinical Pitfall: Subcutaneous nodules are frequently misdiagnosed as tumors (neoplasms).

Ocular Migration & Zoonotic Vector:

Larvae and adult worms of Dirofilaria species can undergo cutaneous or visceral migration. Notably, parasites can migrate to the eye structure causing Ocular Dirofilariasis. Both parasites pose zoonotic risks, making accurate canine screening essential for public health protection.

2. Clinical and Diagnostic Context

Dirofilaria immitis is associated with canine heartworm disease, whereas Dirofilaria repens is associated with subcutaneous dirofilariosis and is zoonotic. The original field research notes that clinical signs may overlap with other parasitic conditions, making species-level differentiation highly relevant in selected cases.

Microscopy Challenges

While microscopic examination is widely taught, reliable morphological differentiation between microfilariae requires substantial expertise and specialized training.

Molecular Testing Integration

Molecular assays (PCR) offer absolute species differentiation, addressing the cross-reactivity and diagnostic ambiguity inherent in standard rapid immunochromatographic assays.

4. Field Observations from Two Veterinary Hospitals

Figure: Clinical Status Quo & Diagnostic Challenges

Hospital A (KOL Clinic)

  • Heavy reliance on combined modified Knott's technique for microfilaria detection.
  • High annual sample throughput due to regional referral status.
  • Emphasis on secondary molecular confirmation when rapid kits show ambiguous results.

Hospital B (General Practice)

  • Rapid antigen cassette testing used as primary point-of-care tool.
  • Limited microscopic species identification due to time/staff constraints.
  • High interest in affordable multiplex kits capable of identifying both species concurrently.

7. Implications & Research Questions

1

Assay Differentiation Need: How can diagnostic manufacturers optimize affordable point-of-care tests to distinguish D. immitis from D. repens without adding extreme cost?

2

Treatment Protocols: Given that D. repens requires different therapeutic management than severe heartworm disease, does non-differentiated screening lead to inappropriate medication regimes?

3

Zoonotic Surveillance: What role should veterinary clinics play in tracking D. repens as a reservoir for human subcutaneous/ocular infections?

8. Limitations

Scope Constraints: This field report is based on observational site visits to two veterinary clinics in Hungary and localized KOL figures.

Data regarding test volumes (600–700 in 2023 to 998 in 2024) reflects site-specific records and should not be generalized to national Hungarian veterinary market totals without broader epidemiological sampling.
