HUNGARY

Canine Dirofilariosis and Molecular Diagnosis

Field Research Report: Clinical Workflows, Species Differentiation, and Market Signals

Field Survey Notes Veterinary Diagnostics Hungary (KOL Clinics)

Executive Summary & Key Diagnostic Challenges

The Hungarian field observations highlight a practical diagnostic challenge in European veterinary clinics. Climate change and the progressive restriction of certain mosquito-control insecticides in the EU over the past decade may have contributed to changing vector exposure patterns, underscoring the need for appropriate diagnostic strategies for canine infections caused by Dirofilaria immitis and Dirofilaria repens. The rapid antigen tests observed during the field visits targeted D. immitis and could not differentiate between the two species, highlighting the complementary role of species-specific diagnostic methods.

The visits also documented substantial use of the modified Knott’s test at one KOL-associated veterinary clinic, with reported annual test volumes increasing from 600–700 in 2023 to 998 in 2024. These observations suggest that, in the current heartworm diagnostic landscape, species differentiation, diagnostic specificity, and standardized testing protocols across clinical settings warrant particular attention. The market opportunity may lie less in pursuing incremental improvements in antigen assay sensitivity and more in developing a practical molecular screening solution that is simple enough for routine veterinary use and capable of addressing diagnostic gaps that antigen-only testing cannot resolve.

1. Overview & Zoonotic Significance

Figure: Diagnostic workflow and chart

Cardiovascular Risk

Dirofilaria immitis

Primary Disease: Heartworm Disease (Canine Heartworm)

  • Pathology: Primarily targets and parasitizes the cardiovascular system.
  • Clinical Impact: Leads to severe cardiovascular disease, pulmonary arterial damage, and heart failure.
Subcutaneous & Zoonotic

Dirofilaria repens

Primary Disease: Subcutaneous Dirofilariasis

  • Pathology: Adult worms parasitize subcutaneous tissues long-term.
  • Symptoms: Multifocal alopecia (patchy hair loss) and skin breakdown/ulceration.
  • Clinical Pitfall: Subcutaneous nodules are frequently misdiagnosed as tumors (neoplasms).

Ocular Migration & Zoonotic Vector:

Larvae and adult worms of Dirofilaria species can undergo cutaneous or visceral migration. Notably, parasites can migrate to the eye structure causing Ocular Dirofilariasis
